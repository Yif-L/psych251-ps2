01 / ASSUME THE NULL

What does a p-value tell you?

Assuming H₀ and the test’s assumptions, p is the probability of a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed.

H₀ is the null model, often “no effect.” A small p says the result is unusual under that model.

It is not P(H₀ is true), the probability your finding is a false positive, or the size or importance of the effect. A large p does not establish “no effect.”

Across valid continuous tests under H₀, p-values are uniform: every equal-width interval is equally likely. With a detectable true effect, more p-values tend to fall near zero; low power can still produce large p-values.

02 / MORE CHANCES TO GET LUCKY

Many tests, more false positives.

A 5% false-positive rate per test does not mean 5% for a whole collection of tests.

For m independent tests with true nulls and α = .05, the chance of at least one false positive is 1 − .95ᵐ.

03 / SELECTING THE WINNING ANALYSIS

How p-hacking creates “discoveries.”

Using the results to choose what to test, keep, or report can turn random noise into an apparently significant finding.

PracticeWhy it raises false positivesBetter approach
Optional stoppingCheck repeatedly; stop at p < .05. Each look creates another opportunity.Prespecify a fixed stopping rule or use a valid sequential design.

Exploration is useful. The problem is presenting a result selected after looking as if it came from one planned test. Valid sequential analysis differs from uncorrected repeated peeking.

04 / CAN YOUR STUDY SEE THE EFFECT?

Power is a detection probability.

Power = P(reject H₀ | a specified true effect), for a particular design and test. It equals 1 − β, where β is the false-negative rate.

Holding other factors fixed, power rises with a larger true effect, larger sample, less noise, or a more lenient α. Raising α also raises false positives.

Low power misses real effects. If publication favors significance, the surviving low-powered findings often exaggerate effects, distorting the literature.

A small pilot gives a noisy effect estimate. Planning from an inflated pilot effect can leave the main study underpowered. Pilots are better for checking feasibility; plan around plausible effects or the smallest effect you care about.

Read: Ch. 10 §10.4 · Sampling slides 17, 31–43

05 / SIZE + UNCERTAINTY

What is the effect, and how precise is it?

An estimate describes the effect’s direction and size. Its confidence interval describes statistical precision.

In the tea example, estimate the treatment effect as mean milk-first rating minus mean tea-first rating. Cohen’s d divides that difference by a pooled standard deviation.

A narrow interval is more precise. A wide interval leaves a wider range of effects compatible with the data and model.

95% confidence means that, over repeated samples, 95% of intervals from this procedure contain the fixed true parameter. It does not assign a 95% probability to this particular interval.

For a matching two-sided test and 95% CI, excluding the null value corresponds to p < .05. Including zero does not prove no effect. CIs do not capture every source of bias.

Read: Ch. 5 §5.2; Ch. 6 §6.5 · Estimation slides 13, 28–45; Inference slides 24–27

06 / COMPARE TWO MODELS

Bayes factors measure relative evidence.

BF₁₀ compares how well H₁ versus H₀ predicts the observed data.

BF₁₀ = P(data | H₁) / P(data | H₀)

BF₁₀ = 5: the data are five times as likely under H₁. BF₁₀ = 0.2: they are five times as likely under H₀. Near 1: little discrimination.

A Bayes factor is not a posterior probability. Posterior odds = BF₁₀ × prior odds.

It depends on the specified models, including parameter priors within them, although it excludes prior odds between models.

Read: Ch. 6 §6.3.1 · Inference slides 45–46; Sampling slides 24–27

Keep these questions separate

ToolQuestion answered
p-valueHow unusual is a statistic this extreme under H₀?
Bayes factorWhich of two specified models predicts these data better, and by how much?
Estimate + CIHow large is the effect, and how precise is our estimate?

A nonsignificant p alone cannot establish H₀. A Bayes factor can favor H₀ relative to a specified H₁. Selecting only favorable BFs can still bias evidence.

RETRIEVAL PRACTICE

Try answering before you reveal.

Practice prompts, not the actual quiz. Explain each answer aloud in one or two sentences.

1. Sketch p-values under H₀ and under a detectable true effect.

H₀: flat across 0–1 for valid continuous tests. True effect: more mass near zero, especially with higher power. A true effect does not guarantee a small p.

2. Twenty true-null tests each use α = .05. Is the overall false-positive risk 5%?

No. For independent tests it is 1 − .95²⁰ ≈ 64%. A per-test error rate differs from the chance of at least one error across the family.

3. A researcher keeps adding participants until p < .05. What went wrong?

The stopping rule depends on the outcome of repeated tests. Ordinary fixed-sample p-values no longer have their promised error control. Prespecify stopping or use valid sequential methods.

4. Why can a published significant effect from a small study be exaggerated?

The estimate is noisy. If only significance gets published, unusually large estimates preferentially survive. That is selection bias, not evidence that small samples cause larger true effects.

5. An effect CI is [−0.1, 0.8]. Does that prove no effect?

No. Zero and positive effects are compatible with this interval. It is relatively imprecise. A CI containing zero fails to exclude the null in the matching test; it does not establish it.

6. BF₁₀ = 4. Does H₁ have an 80% probability?

Only if prior odds are 1:1. The BF alone says the observed data are four times as likely under H₁. With prior odds of 1:9, posterior odds are 4:9, so P(H₁ | data) = 4/13.
